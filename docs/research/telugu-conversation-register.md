# Telugu conversational register

Reviewed: 2026-08-02

This brief is the language policy for PracticalTelugu's phrase cards and Practice Live. It is intentionally a beginner-safe product policy, not a claim that every Telugu-speaking family or region follows one rigid social rule.

## Product rule in one sentence

Use respectful Telugu for an elder or anyone you do not know well, even if they are your age; use familiar Telugu with a sibling, close friend, child, younger relative, or a person with whom familiarity is clearly mutual.

Age matters, but it is not the whole distinction. Relationship, familiarity, status, number, household convention, and the situation all contribute. A learner is socially safer starting respectfully and moving to familiar speech after the relationship licenses it.

## The beginner-safe system

| Meaning | Someone close | Elder or someone new |
|---|---|---|
| you | `nuvvu` | `meeru` |
| your | `nee` | `mee` |
| how are you? | `elaa unnaavu?` | `elaa unnaaru?` |
| are you doing well? | `baagunnaavaa?` | `baagunnaaraa?` |
| have you eaten? | `tinnaavaa?` | `tinnaaraa?` |
| tell me | `cheppu` | `cheppandi` |
| say it again | `malli cheppu` | `malli cheppandi` |
| say it slowly | `mellagaa cheppu` | `mellagaa cheppandi` |
| what is your name? | `nee peru enti?` | `mee peru enti?` |

`meeru` is both respectful singular and ordinary plural. The respectful/plural agreement continues into verbs, so changing only the pronoun is not enough. The UC Davis grammar explicitly treats `meeru` as the normal choice for seniors and peers and treats `nuvvu` as intimate/familiar; it also requires matching verb agreement. See [Vocatives, Honor Conventions and Interjections](https://www.cs.ucdavis.edu/~vemuri/Grammar/11.%20vocatives.pdf) and [A Grammar of Modern Telugu](https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.219638/2015.219638.A-Grammar_djvu.txt).

### What the two card labels mean

- **Someone close** means a real relationship: a sibling, close friend, child, younger relative, or a person who already speaks mutually familiar Telugu with the learner. Being the same age by itself is not enough.
- **Elder or someone new** is the safe default for an elder, teacher, colleague not yet close, shopkeeper, driver, server, or any unfamiliar adult. It also works for a group.
- **Works with anyone** means the phrase itself does not carry a listener-specific pronoun, ending, or imperative. Tone and surrounding words can still affect politeness.

## Where social meaning lives

### Pronouns and possessives

`nuvvu / nee` and `meeru / mee` make the familiar-respectful contrast visible. The Telugu-English dictionary defines `meeru` as polite singular or plural; modern grammars warn that pronoun choice carries social value. [Gwynn dictionary: మీరు](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=%E0%B0%AE%E0%B1%80%E0%B0%B0%E0%B1%81&searchhws=yes)

### Verb agreement

The contrast often remains even when Telugu drops the pronoun:

- `ekkadunnaavu?` — familiar
- `ekkadunnaaru?` — respectful

Telugu is a consistent null-subject language with rich agreement, and pronoun dropping is common in speech. That means a missing `nuvvu` or `meeru` must not be classified by guesswork; the verb or the complete context carries the signal. [Kansas Working Papers in Linguistics](https://journals.ku.edu/kwpl/article/view/17218)

Some fragments do not carry a decisive listener register at all. `ekkadi nunchi?` can work without an explicit pronoun because the person is already clear. It should not be automatically labeled familiar merely because a longer `meeru` version also exists.

### Requests and imperatives

The respectful imperative commonly uses `-andi`:

- `cheppu` / `cheppandi`
- `raa` / `randi`
- `koorcho` / `koorchondi`
- `po` / `pondi`

For strangers and service encounters, the respectful form is the beginner-safe default. A driver-facing phrase such as `ikkada aapandi` is already polite because of its ending; an additional word equivalent to "please" is not required for every natural request. [Gwynn dictionary: అండి](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=%E0%B0%85%E0%B0%82%E0%B0%A1%E0%B0%BF&searchhws=yes)

`andi` also appears as a polite conversational particle, as in `avunandi`, `kaadandi`, or an attention-getting `emandii`. It should be introduced as a flexible respect marker, not translated mechanically as "sir" or "madam" in every sentence.

### Honorifics and terms of address

`gaaru` can follow a name, relationship term, or profession to show respect. `anna` and `akka` can warmly address a man or woman in everyday service or street contexts, even without a literal sibling relationship, but they are relational choices rather than universal replacements for politeness. [Gwynn dictionary: గారు](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=%E0%B0%97%E0%B0%BE%E0%B0%B0%E0%B1%81&searchhws=yes), [అన్న](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=%E0%B0%85%E0%B0%A8%E0%B1%8D%E0%B0%A8&searchhws=yes), [అక్క](https://dsal.uchicago.edu/cgi-bin/app/gwynn_query.py?qs=%E0%B0%85%E0%B0%95%E0%B1%8D%E0%B0%95&searchhws=yes)

The course should not teach `orey`, `osey`, `evadu`, `evate`, or `tamaru` as beginner defaults. The first four can be insulting or extremely intimate in the wrong context; `tamaru` is unusually deferential and can sound theatrical, mocking, or unnatural when misused. The UC Davis vocatives chapter explicitly warns learners about these choices.

### Respect toward a person being discussed

Not every respectful ending targets the listener. In `amma intlo unnaaraa?`, the plural/respectful agreement honors Amma, the person being discussed. The learner can use that sentence while speaking to a close sibling. Product metadata therefore distinguishes listener register from respect toward a third-person referent.

## Conversational forms, not textbook-only forms

PracticalTelugu keeps common spoken forms as the main learning target:

- `enti` is the common conversational contraction of fuller `emiti`.
- `parledu` is the common spoken form beside fuller `paravaaledu`.
- Telugu frequently omits a recoverable pronoun or subject.
- A short fragment such as `inkonchem` or `chaalu` can be natural at a family table; tone and situation carry much of the politeness.

The older grammar sources often document conservative full forms. They establish the morphology and register contrast, while contemporary conversational evidence informs which form appears first in this product. The modern teaching site [Matladu](https://matladu.in/) independently uses a relationship-first register model and meaning-first Roman Telugu; it is useful practitioner evidence, not a substitute for a grammar or dictionary.

### Family hunger follow-up

The tested family dialogue exposed a specific model error worth making explicit. "I am still hungry" is `naaku inkaa aakaligaa undi` (`నాకు ఇంకా ఆకలిగా ఉంది`), not `pasigaa undi`; `pasi` ordinarily describes something little, tender, or young. A natural next question is `avunaa? inkaa emainaa tintaavaa?` for someone close or `avunaa? inkaa emainaa tintaaraa?` respectfully. The hunger construction is attested in an [IGNOU Telugu course](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/85946/1/Block-1.pdf), while the lexical sense of [`pasi`](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E0%B0%AA%E0%B0%B8%E0%B0%BF) confirms why the model's substitution was wrong.

## Content decisions for the current course

1. Keep every existing stable progress key, word ID, and alternative order.
2. Mark familiar and respectful forms semantically rather than inferring them from free-text labels.
3. Show the primary form's context as well as the alternative's context. A learner must be able to compare `nee peru enti?` and `mee peru enti?` without opening a note.
4. Replace age-only guidance with relationship-first guidance.
5. Mark respectful service imperatives as polite with a stranger.
6. Keep style differences such as spoken/full/formal separate from listener register.
7. Treat `amma intlo unnaaraa?` as respect toward Amma rather than evidence that the listener is an elder.
8. Preserve native Telugu forms already chosen for hello, thanks, apology, and recovery instead of reintroducing English loanword stand-ins.
9. Validate high-confidence live dialogue anchors instead of relying on a system prompt alone; current guards cover copied-English fillers and the verified hunger exchange.

## Practice Live register contract

Before a voice session, the learner chooses one listener:

- **Someone close**: Mayu uses and elicits `nuvvu`, `nee`, familiar verb agreement, and familiar imperatives.
- **Elder or someone new**: Mayu uses and elicits `meeru`, `mee`, respectful `-aaru` agreement, and `-andi` imperatives.

The respectful choice is the default. Within one exchange, Mayu must not mix the two systems. Pronouns may be dropped naturally, but the remaining agreement must still match. Third-person honorific agreement is allowed when the person being discussed warrants it.

If the learner uses the other register, Mayu should understand the meaning, model one matching form naturally, and continue. It should not turn a conversation into a grammar lecture or shame the learner.

## Voice timing and spend policy

Practice Live offers a one-minute default and a two-minute option. The browser receives a one-use ephemeral credential only after microphone permission succeeds. The requested duration is validated by the server, returned as the authoritative deadline, and paired with a short token lifetime. This follows Google's guidance to use restricted ephemeral tokens for direct low-latency browser connections and to keep their expiration short. [Gemini ephemeral tokens](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api/ephemeral-tokens)

The existing audio pipeline already follows Google's important low-latency practices: direct client-to-Gemini transport, roughly 20–40 ms input chunks, 16 kHz input resampling, 24 kHz playback, immediate interruption buffer clearing, and minimal thinking. The remaining deliberate latency comes from end-of-speech detection and the blocking caption tool that guarantees readable Telugu and English meaning before audio. [Gemini Live best practices](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/live-api/best-practices)

The caption-before-audio contract remains a product priority. It is more useful to a beginner than shaving latency by letting speech arrive without the promised reading support. The low-risk latency change is a 500 ms silence threshold plus a more concise system/tool prompt; a different asynchronous caption architecture needs a measured A/B test.

## Visual direction for the voice orb

The visual reference is ElevenLabs' restraint, not a literal clone. ElevenLabs exposes two orb colors in its widget and its open UI component treats the orb as one coherent, audio-reactive material. PracticalTelugu should likewise use two adjacent peacock-blue/teal tones, one light source, gentle depth, smooth amplitude response, and state motion that reads as breathing rather than three unrelated colored blobs. [ElevenLabs widget customization](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/eleven-agents/customization/widget), [ElevenLabs UI repository](https://github.com/elevenlabs/ui)

The implementation remains lightweight CSS rather than adding a 3D rendering stack solely for this component. Reduced-motion users keep the same state clarity without continuous morphing.

## Source limits

- Grammar and dictionary sources are strong for pronouns, agreement, imperatives, and honorific morphology; some are older and describe social hierarchies that should not be copied as modern product norms.
- Contemporary conversational sources are better for frequency and product framing, but are less authoritative about morphology.
- Household and regional usage varies. A respectful default is a risk-minimizing beginner policy, not a judgment about families that use familiar forms with parents or grandparents.
- `dhanyavaadaalu`, `kshaminchandi`, and fixed meeting formulas have less direct support in the core register sources than the pronoun/verb system. The app presents them conservatively and explains when they sound deliberate or formal.
